<h2 class="research-title"><span class="research-number">01</span> Humidity as a Positional Cue</h2>

<p>Plant epidermal identity is developmentally determined through a dedicated epidermal lineage. Under specific conditions, such as wounding, internal cells can undergo transdifferentiation to acquire an epidermal cell fate(<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-025-02028-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Lee et al., 2025</a>). Remarkably, even then, only the outermost cell layer acquires epidermal identity. This observation led to the concept of a <em>positional cue</em>, although its nature has remained elusive (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7913478/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Iida and Takada, 2021</a>).</p>

<p>Our recent work showed that high humidity disrupts the maintenance of epidermal identity. This finding suggests that an positional cue is interpreted, at least in part, through a desiccation-associated cue: a cell recognizes that it is no longer in a persistently hydrated internal environment (<a href="https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(25)01479-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jeon et al., 2026</a>). We are now investigating how plants sense humidity and convert this environmental input into signals that specify epidermal cell fate.</p>
</section>

<section class="research-section">
<h2 class="research-title"><span class="research-number">02</span> Carapace Lignin: A Novel Epidermis-Specific Lignification</h2>

<p>Humidity also revealed an unexpected mode of epidermal differentiation. When positional cue is compromised and cuticle formation fails, lignin is deposited specifically in the outermost cell layer—the position normally occupied by the canonical cuticle (<a href="https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(25)01479-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jeon et al., 2026</a>). We designated this previously unrecognized epidermal lignification <em>carapace lignin</em>. The name is inspired by the turtle carapace, an ancestral endoskeletal structure that became positioned at the body surface during evolution (<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms3107" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Hirasawa et al., 2013</a>).</p>

<p>Carapace lignin suggests that epidermal identity is not governed by humidity alone. Rather, outermost cells may integrate both humidity-dependent and humidity-independent positional information. We are investigating how distinct cell types interpret these inputs, distinguishing cells that integrate both cues from those whose epidermal specification depends predominantly on humidity. In parallel, we are identifying the molecular mechanisms underlying carapace lignin formation.</p>
</section>

<section class="research-section">
<h2 class="research-title"><span class="research-number">03</span> Abscission as a Developmental Strategy in Trees</h2>

<p>Like lizards that shed their tails through autotomy, plants can actively discard organs through abscission. Traditionally, abscission has been viewed primarily as a terminal event—for example, autumn leaf drop, petal shedding after fertilization, or fruit release at maturity. Our work instead highlights abscission as an active developmental strategy that can optimize reproduction and protect future growth.</p>

<p>In cherry (<em>Prunus</em>), we identified five sequential abscission events—petal, calyx, flower pedicel, fruit pedicel, and peduncle abscission—that together form a hierarchical reproductive program (<a href="https://academic.oup.com/hr/article/13/2/uhaf317/8323362" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jeon et al., 2026</a>). Developmentally arrested pre-formed abscission zones, together with a <em>de-novo</em> formed abscission zone, are activated by localized signaling. Their activation involves coordinated cell differentiation and cell-wall remodeling, including lignification. By sequentially eliminating unfertilized flowers and underdeveloped fruits, this program progressively refines reproductive investment and final fruit set.</p>

<p>In maple (<em>Acer</em>), the strategy is fundamentally different. Through marcescence, senesced leaves remain attached throughout winter rather than being shed in autumn, extending their role as protective structures around developing buds. Thus, cherry uses abscission to selectively regulate reproductive output, whereas maple delays abscission to safeguard future growth. Together, these systems show how the same developmental process can be repurposed for distinct adaptive outcomes in woody plants.</p>
</section>

<section class="research-section">
<h2 class="research-title"><span class="research-number">04</span> Breaking Ground: Cell-Wall Remodeling for Root Penetration</h2>

<p>Plant cell walls are specialized to support distinct biological functions. The cuticle, for example, is typically formed on aerial organs, where it limits water loss and pathogen entry. Unexpectedly, a cuticle structure—the root cap cuticle (RCC)—also forms at the root tip during embryogenesis (<a href="https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)30038-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Berhin et al., 2019</a>). RCC contributes to osmotic-stress tolerance and lateral-root emergence.</p>

<p>Because RCC supports lateral roots as they penetrate internal tissues, we asked whether it also contributes to the fundamental task of primary-root penetration through soil. We found that RCC is required for efficient root penetration and further influences root-cap detachment and gravitropic responses (<a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/pce.70622" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jeon and Kim et al., 2026</a>).</p>

<p>These findings indicate that the cuticle is not merely a passive mechanical barrier. It is intimately connected to both physical force generation and developmental signaling. Our current research examines: (i) how RCC-derived structural information is converted into cellular signals, and (ii) how other secondary cell-wall modifications, including ligno-suberization, contribute to root penetration.</p>
</section>
